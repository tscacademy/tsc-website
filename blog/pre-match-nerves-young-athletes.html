Pre-Match Nerves in Young Athletes: A Parent's Guide

Why kids freeze in big matches, and a simple pre-match routine and mistake reset your athlete can start using this week.

TSC Academy coaching team · November 27, 2026 · 2 min read

Your athlete plays brilliantly in practice. Then the match that matters arrives and they look like a different player: tight, hesitant, rushing. If that sounds familiar, you are not dealing with a lack of talent. You are dealing with pressure, and pressure is a skill that can be trained.

Why nerves show up on match day

Nerves are the body getting ready for something that feels important. Heart rate goes up, breathing gets shallow, muscles tighten. A little of that helps performance. Too much, and fine skills (a second serve, a first touch, a free throw) get worse.

Young athletes often read those signals as "something is wrong with me". That thought adds a second layer of stress on top of the first. The goal is not to make nerves disappear. It is to help your athlete expect them, name them and use them.

A 3-step pre-match routine

A routine gives the mind something familiar to hold on to when everything else feels new.

  1. 1. Breathe low and slow (60 seconds). In for 4 counts through the nose, out for 6 through the mouth. A longer exhale is one of the quickest ways to calm the body down.
  2. 2. One cue word. Pick a short word linked to how they want to play: "smooth", "attack", "first step". Say it before warm-up and before the first point or kick-off.
  3. 3. See the first moment. Eyes closed for 20 seconds, they picture the first serve or first touch going well, from their own eyes, hearing the sounds of the venue.

Practice the routine at training for a week before using it in a match. A routine that only appears on match day feels strange, not safe.

The 20-second reset after a mistake

Most matches are not lost on the first mistake. They are lost on the next three, when the athlete is still thinking about the first one. Teach a reset:

  • Exhale long, once.
  • A physical gesture that means "done": touch the strings, fix the socks, wipe the hands.
  • The cue word again, and attention back to the next action.
The best athletes do not make fewer mistakes. They recover faster.

What parents can do

  • Before the match, keep talk short and about effort and the routine, not results or rankings.
  • During the match, stay calm and quiet. Kids read the sideline more than you think.
  • After the match, wait. Let them speak first, and keep the "analysis" for the next day, if at all.

When to get help

If nerves are getting worse over months, if your athlete avoids competing, or if worry shows up outside sport too (sleep, school, appetite), talk to your pediatrician or a licensed mental health professional. Performance coaching is about skills for competing. It is not a replacement for clinical care.
