Is Strength Training Safe for Kids? What Parents Should Know

Supervised strength training is widely considered safe and useful for kids and teens. How to start, what to avoid, and how it helps young athletes.

TSC Academy coaching team · November 24, 2026 · 1 min read

"Lifting weights will stunt their growth." Many parents still hear this. The evidence says otherwise: major pediatric and sports medicine organizations consider properly supervised strength training safe and beneficial for children and adolescents.

Why strength matters for young athletes

  • Fewer injuries. Stronger muscles and tendons handle the forces of jumping, cutting and landing better.
  • Better performance. Speed, jumping and change of direction all depend on force.
  • Better movement. Strength training teaches control of the body, which carries over to technique.
  • Confidence. Kids see clear progress, which is motivating.

What "strength training" means for a 10-year-old

It does not mean maximal lifts in a gym. It means learning to move well under control:

  • Squats, lunges, hinges and push-ups with body weight
  • Planks, side planks and carries for the trunk
  • Jumps and landings, starting small
  • Light external load (bands, medicine balls, light dumbbells) once technique is solid

Simple rules

  1. 1. Technique first, load later. Add weight only when the movement looks clean.
  2. 2. Qualified supervision. A coach who knows youth training.
  3. 3. Progress slowly. Small increases, plenty of recovery.
  4. 4. 2–3 short sessions a week is enough for most young athletes.
  5. 5. Stop if it hurts. Discomfort from effort is fine. Sharp or joint pain is not.
The risk is not strength training. The risk is poorly supervised training, bad technique, or too much too soon.

What about growth plates?

Injuries to growth plates in youth strength training are rare and are usually linked to poor technique, lack of supervision or maximal lifts. With a sensible program, strength training can actually help bones become stronger.

How to start

Begin with body weight, twice a week, 20–30 minutes, focusing on quality. Re-test simple things (jump height, plank time, push-ups) every 4–6 weeks so your athlete can see progress.
